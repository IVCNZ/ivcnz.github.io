Seeing With, Not Seeing For: Computer Vision and Indigenous Knowledge

Computer vision is changing how we observe the world, from remote-sensing imagery that tracks shifting coastlines to algorithms that can sort millions of archival photographs with speed. For Indigenous communities, these tools carry both promise and an old danger. The camera has long been an instrument of extraction: ethnographic photography, museum collections, and surveillance technologies have captured Indigenous peoples, lands, and sacred objects without consent and removed them from the contexts that give them meaning. As machine learning systems are trained on these same images, they risk automating that history at scale.

This keynote provides an avenue for computer vision to also be a tool of Indigenous self-determination, but only when Indigenous communities are involved in the conceptual, design, augmentation, and evaluation stages of its creation. Drawing on the veracity approach being developed within the Hiringa Te Mahara research program, I speak to emerging topics in computer vision through the lens of Indigenous-led research. Examples include XR (VR and AR), multispectral imaging, convolutional neural networks, and adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems as technologies that offer ways to preserve heritage and support environmental monitoring of land, sea, and flora.

The central claim is simple: a practical computer vision research strategy depends not on a better algorithm, but on better Indigenous processing of context when framing research with communities, stronger relationships with those communities, engagement in ways that are familiar to them during the construction of these technologies, and evaluation of their efficacy on those same terms.

Kevin Shedlock

Ngāpuhi, Ngāti Porou, Whakatōhea

Dr Kevin Shedlock is a Lecturer in Computer Science at Te Herenga Waka - Victoria University of Wellington. His doctoral research examined Indigenous knowledge systems and the construction of virtual reality, and he currently works with Māori and North American Indigenous communities to build technology through an Indigenous lens.

His partnership with Te Ruapekapeka Trust used machine learning, virtual reality and augmented reality to preserve and share Māori knowledge. His wider research supports Māori communities in adopting immersive, intelligent and interactive virtual technologies. He is a key researcher with the international Abundant Intelligences research program and leads the Hiringa Te Mahara research program in Aotearoa. A prominent voice on AI in Aotearoa, Dr Shedlock argues that Māori must be at the centre of deciding its future, with authority over information about their people, communities, and knowledge.

Beyond Accuracy: What It Takes for Medical Imaging AI to Succeed in Clinical Practice

Medical imaging plays a central role in many areas of healthcare, from diagnosis and risk assessment to treatment planning, delivery, and response evaluation. At the same time, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are creating new opportunities to improve efficiency, support clinical decision-making, and enable more personalized care. However, only a limited fraction of research innovations ultimately reach routine clinical practice.

In this keynote, I will use radiotherapy as a primary case study to illustrate where medical imaging and AI can have meaningful clinical impact. Drawing on examples from radiotherapy as well as other healthcare applications, I will highlight practical lessons learned regarding evaluation, explainability, user acceptance, and workflow integration. I will discuss what is needed for research to move beyond promising technical results towards genuine clinical adoption and improved patient care.

Tanja Alderliesten

Prof. dr. Tanja Alderliesten is Professor of Translational Artificial Intelligence in Radiotherapy at Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), The Netherlands, where she leads the AI-Based Innovations research group. Her research focuses on advancing personalized healthcare through the development, evaluation, and implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, with a particular emphasis on medical imaging and cancer care.

Working at the intersection of medical imaging, computer science, mathematics, and oncology, she has more than 20 years of experience in medical imaging and AI research, including over 15 years in radiation oncology. Her work spans medical image analysis, treatment optimization, explainable AI, and clinical decision support, with a strong focus on translating methodological innovations into clinically deployable solutions. Through multidisciplinary collaborations with clinicians, medical physicists, computer scientists, mathematicians, and industry partners, she pursues research in which clinical needs drive methodological innovation and solutions are co-created to support meaningful adoption and implementation in clinical practice.

Her research has contributed to advances in image-guided and adaptive radiotherapy, brachytherapy, medical image analysis, explainable AI, and AI-supported clinical decision-making. While much of her work is rooted in oncology, she also contributes to collaborative research initiatives developing and translating AI technologies for broader healthcare challenges, including cardiovascular disease and primary care.

From Pixels to Poles: GeoCam's Vision-Based Pipeline for Mapping Infrastructure

Mapping roads, utility networks, and facilities at scale is as much a systems problem as a vision problem. This keynote walks through GeoCam's integrated platform, starting with our purpose-built camera rigs with RTK GNSS and IMU, which capture across street, walking, indoor, and aerial modes. We'll look at how we fuse these captures into a consistent scene using ML, SfM, and SLAM, and the tools we use to create GIS data. We'll discuss some of the problems that arise in a range of environments, and the many lessons learned.

Philip McLeod

Dr Philip McLeod completed his PhD from the University of Otago in 2008 on Signal processing and Pitch Detection. After a short stint of lecturing, Phil worked in the games industry focused on image based rendering. In 2016, Phil co-founded GeoCam and is the lead developer of their Visual Positioning System.
